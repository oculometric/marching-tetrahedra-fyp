# Introduction
This paper compares several variations in approach to extraction of isosurfaces from 3-dimensional scalar functions. It evaluates the demonstrated algorithms for their fitness specifically as applied to real-time procedural mesh generation for non-height-map terrain in videogames. !EXPLAIN PROBLEM! This places a number of restrictions and goals on the algorithm used: !MOVE THIS STUFF TO LATER ON, SUMMARISE INSTEAD!
1. The algorithm must be able to generate a mesh directly from a provided scalar function.
2. The algorithm should generate manifold, closed geometry.
3. The algorithm should be highly optimised for speed, with minimal startup time.
4. The algorithm should produce geometry with minimal triangle count.
5. The algorithm should produce geometry which avoids shading artifacts when using smooth shading such as Phong. !CITATION!
For real-time rendering applications such as videogames, triangle counts should be reduced where possible to minimise GPU utilisation. Since terrain meshes often take on organic forms !CITATION!, they can benefit greatly from regularisation techniques which are able to distribute triangles and eliminate small faces. Extremely small or narrow faces which often result from isosurface extraction algorithms are wasteful both due to overall triangle count, and as a result of overdraw. Nearly all modern graphics acceleration hardware shades pixels in $2\times2$ chunks, often called 'quads' !CITATION!. Overdraw occurs when wasted work is performed due to a triangle only covering a portion of a quad, resulting in pixels being shaded many more times than is necessary and contributing to worse performance. The presence of tiny or elongated triangles in an organic mesh also results in shading problems !DEMONSTRATE WITH A PAIR OF IMAGES! which significantly degrade the appearance of geometry shaded using interpolated normals (such as Phong).

In addition to this, the performance of the isosurface extraction algorithm is of great importance. Many videogames involve player-modifiable worlds, which may include terrain which is initially procedurally generated, such as !ASTRONEER CITATION!. In order to facilitate this, two considerations are made: first, that the algorithm should be capable of evaluating a usefully-sized volume of space within a typical game frame-time (1/60 of a second); second, that the algorithm should be capable of evaluating a larger volume as a series of smaller blocks if necessary. Modifiable terrain is unlikely to be altered in many places at once, and thus not all of the space must be re-tessellated for every modification.

The final key consideration for the algorithm is that it reliably produces manifold, closed geometry. A manifold (in this case, a 2-manifold) implies a surface which does not intersect itself, where its surface is appears locally Euclidian at all points !CITATION!. For videogames, these restrictions produce a mesh which does not appear to cross itself (which would usually be considered non-physical and thus inappropriate for virtual terrain), which does not instantaneously invert its orientation (normal vectors vary smoothly across the surface), which has a defined inside and outside (as conveyed by the direction of the surface normals), and which has no loose geometry or holes, except where the evaluation space terminates. All of these conditions are necessary to produce geometry which may be perceived by players as physically solid, as single-sided holes, self-intersections, and surfaces viewable from unexpected angles (due to inverted normal vectors) all break the illusion of a solid object.

This paper examines the literature in relation to relevant isosurface extraction methods, and will implement the landmark 'regularised marching tetrahedra' algorithm presented by Treece et. al. An alternative arrangement of the tetrahedral lattice, and an alternative mesh simplification algorithm are presented and the impact of these variations is examined with regard to the evaluation time, triangle count and mean aspect ratio, and perceived visual quality of resulting meshes. !REORDER THIS AND MOVE OTHER PARAS TO TECHNICAL CONSIDERATIONS!

# Literature Review
## Isosurfaces
A significant volume of research has been conducted on the subject of isosurface extraction, predominantly for the purpose of visualising medical imaging results. An early example is Chen et al., 1985, which considers the problem of rendering CT scan results into smooth 3D surfaces, without large computational or storage costs. It bypasses the isosurface representation and produces a shaded image directly from the 'cuberille' voxel data.
The term 'isosurface extraction' refers to the construction of a geometric surface in *n*-dimensional space filled with a scalar field, such that all points on that surface have the same value in the scalar field. For example, for a scalar field defined by the formula $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the manifold representing an isovalue of $1$ would be a circle, of radius $1$, centered on the origin.
## Marching Cubes
A cornerstone algorithm for purposes here is the marching cubes algorithm, presented in Lorensen and Cline, 1998. Also developed for medical imaging purposes, marching cubes produces triangle geometry for small blocks of the sample space one-by-one, and then *marches* to the next block. Each block is evaluated according to whether each corner of the currently considered cube is inside or outside the isosurface (that is to say, whether the value at each corner is less than or greater than the chosen isovalue); the eight boolean corner states is then used to index into a table of predefined geometries. !DIAGRAM! The table of potential geometry configurations is quite large; initially on the order of 256 ($2^8$), it can be reduced to just 14 unique cases via symmetry and rotations. Vertex positions are then interpolated such that they lie on the isosurface.

This divide-and-conquer approach simplifies the problem and enables parallelisation due to the fact that individual cubes of the sample space are entirely independent (although it is worth noting that there s some shared computation on the boundary between two cubes), and is an excellent general-purpose approach. However, the original marching cubes algorithm has ambiguous geometry for some configurations (Chan and Purisima, 1998) !DIAGRAM!. Several improvements to the original marching cubes algorithm have been proposed to address this, such as !TWO CITATIONS WITH SMALL EXPLANATIONS!.

Marching cubes also produces a large number of triangles, many of which are of poor quality !GLOSSARY/INTRO TERM! (as described above, meaning they are highly elongated or are extremely small), which results in poor quality shading and high storage and rendering cost.
## Marching Tetrahedra
The marching tetrahedra algorithm is an evolution of marching cubes presented by !CITATION! which eliminates the ambiguity problem as a result of reducing the number of corners involved in a single primitive from eight to four; at the same time reducing the number of configurations per cell to just 16 ($2^4$), or 3 when considering symmetry and rotations. These per-tetrahedron configurations consist of only 2, 1, 0 triangles each. !SHOW DIAGRAMS OF THE DIFFERENT CONFIGURATIONS!

When tessellating 3-dimensional space with tetrahedra, the arrangement must be considered. The three simplest arrangements are simple cubic (!DIAGRAM!), face-centered (!DIAGRAM!), and body-centered (!DIAGRAM!). It has been demonstrated that the body-centered lattice tessellation contains tetrahedra which have edge length ratios close to $1$, meaning they produce triangles closest to equilateral, and that this tessellation also has a higher sampling efficiency than other arrangements (Chan and Purisima, 1998); hence many MT implementations use this arrangement.

However, marching tetrahedra generally produces worse meshes than marching cubes (Treece, Prager, and Gee, 1999) with a larger number of tiny or elongated triangles, which are undesirable. As such, both algorithms demand a simplification or regularisation mechanism to improve the final mesh. Simplification methods are discussed in more detail below.
## Regularised Marching Tetrahedra
RMT, presented by Treece, Prager, and Gee, 1999, improves the marching tetrahedra algorithm by integrating the simplification process with the geometry generation process itself. RMT performs an intermediate vertex clustering procedure, eliminating the need for an expensive edge-collapse algorithm to be applied afterwards, and taking advantage of the topological context which is only available during geometry generation itself. Each sample point is first determined to be either inside or outside the isosurface, and candidate vertices are positioned along edges which connect sample points, using interpolation to align them with the isosurface, as with marching cubes. Then, candidate vertices are grouped according to which sample point they are closest to. The connectivity of these vertices are considered, and a subset of them may be merged together into a single vertex. Consideration of the connectivity allows the merging process to preserve topological features, such as closed surfaces, loops, or disconnected pieces. The triangle mesh is then constructed in a later step using the vertices computed previously, using the same per-tetrahedron table of possible geometries as the existing marching tetrahedra algorithm. Triangles which contain more than one copy of the same vertex - and thus would be 'degenerate', having zero area - are discarded. !DIAGRAM FOR THE RMT MERGING ALGORITHM; PSEUDOCODE?! This approach massively reduces the total number of vertices and triangles in the mesh, and eliminates tiny or highly elongated triangles, while preserving topological details and mesh integrity.
